I don't know why I started a relationship with Anhela. Indeed, it seems that we both didn't know why we were together, although we lasted a few months. Yes, she was pretty, yes, she let me live at her place and escape my parents, but that was it. It was a good deal, and the philosophy of Strugački, our boss (and, by extension, of Anhela), was never to give up on good deals. But it was a deal with the machine, as every deal was—a deal with the devil, if you must (God doesn't do deals).

Actually, we were spending so much time together working that we became friends just based on the jokes about Strugački that we were sharing. We were thinking of ways to win some free time from him, e.g. we would set up a meeting for discussing some feature, but we would go get coffee instead. Then she started inviting me to her place and I decided that I would be a fool not to accept (see, the "don't be a fool" philosophy in action).

The first time we had sex was very awkward, and not only because we were inexperienced; it was as if you were trying to connect two incompatible pieces of equipment. But in the end we somehow succeeded (you can bond with anybody, if you really try) and the whole thing felt like magic. We had some good sex and after that I really didn't feel like leaving for "my" place.

>I know that nothing's truly ours, but still it makes us feel better if we have the papers.
{: .note}

It was very different when I actually came with my suitcases to move in with her. I saw her place, and the situation itself, in a different light: her furniture was all new, but very basic, with nothing that left any impression. The walls were white, empty. She practically lived in an office. Even the pens were all branded.

In other words, I understood that I had to THINK about my decision to live with her. With my 20-something brain, I decided that after L. was dead, my chances of feeling true love again were gone, and so something like Anhela was indeed the best I could hope for. And she indeed was the best that I could hope for at that time; later I just learned how to hope better. But at that time, I was still basically looking at how jealous my life made the people around me. And Anhela DID make everyone I cared about very jealous.

My parents were stumped. My whole relationship with them was based on the idea that I was fragile and insecure and that their mission was to make me less fragile and insecure so that one day I could find a good job and a good partner. And then suddenly I found both of these things. They almost didn't know what to say to me, besides warning me how hard relationships (and jobs) were.

"And remember that you will always be welcome here."
{: .voice-father}

It sounded a little too much like a wish.

And then there was Mirko, who was similarly jealous. He had always been the smarter and harder-working one. And then suddenly I was his boss and was dating the most beautiful girl in the building. Like me, he was never into these kinds of things, but still it was reasonable for him to expect to get them eventually, given that he had dedicated his life to work and to being serious (unlike me, who dedicated it to... nothing in particular, I guess?), and when he didn't, he blamed it on me. The situation became especially bad once I started taking Anhela to our lunches—at first he started making fun of her behind her back, about how clueless she was, and how she knew more about makeup than technology, and then, when he realized that I was laughing with him, he went on to make fun of me for prostituting myself to ugly women for promotions. This would have offended me, but we both knew that Anhela wasn't ugly, so it was plain to see he was just trying to make me feel bad. In short, we finally started behaving like adults—we managed to get into fights over things that none of us cared about. And I lost hope of ever coming to terms with him so that we could talk about the phenomenon. Not that there was anything to talk about—ever since I had invested myself in my work I never felt its presence at all.

But that was normal. My job was soul-crushing, and Anhela wasn't very supportive either. I didn't want to tell it to Mirko, but she indeed was an expert on makeup—she spent hours and hours preparing for work and then, in the evening, preparing for bedtime, which ate up all the time when we could, theoretically talk or do something else together, although it seemed that once the honeymoon had passed, and she decided that she had aquired me as a partner (not permanetly, but this was never her intention) she can resume living her life as before and use me only when she felt like it.

I began this chapter by saying I don't know why I started this relationship with Anhela, when I didn't really like her. I think that the reason was that I was in dire need to like *something*. Boredom often forces us to trade something that has real intrinsic value for us for something that has merely extrinsic (instrumental) value. It makes us desire, if not happiness, at least the appearance, the promise of happiness.

This was the big scam that the machine pulls (the same old scam that the devil uses to get your soul in the old parable): it made me trade the things that were of real value (hope) for a mere appearance of happiness, for the promise that happiness will come if I continue following its path. It was true that I had always envisioned happiness this way—me, standing next to a beautiful woman, writing Haskell for a living (and making a lot of money, why not?)—but what could I do when I had all of this and wasn't really happy? I could not just abandon it all (at least not yet). And my new job was consuming me, so I could not do anything else, and all I was left with was the promise. All that I could do was *wait*, wait for me to be happy, wait for things to get better. It (the machine) had made me a slave to the future, a waiter. A happy waiter (one that constantly keeps the image of a bright future in their head), but a waiter nonetheless.

>Become an expert in moral philosophy.
{: .note}

In short, I traded the readiness to get love for some temporary gratification in a nice clean apartment. The only good thing was that I managed to get away from my parents for some time, but that was also temporary.

---

The unhappier I was, the more I was envied by the people around me. OK, actually I have to admit I made them, Mirko specifically, envy me intentionally: I got this weird idea from Anhela that appearing happy is a prerequisite of being really happy, that acting as if something is true would make it really so (the "fake it till you make it" mantra, as you may know it). I honestly don't remember in what context this can possibly be true, or why I believed it. I mean, I know, and I knew then as well, that, for example, you don't become a hacker by just taking a keyboard and typing really fast into it (or you don't become an IT project manager by whatever it was that Anhela was doing).

Or perhaps I made Mirko envy me because I subconsciously wanted something to change—I subconsciously tried to make him do what he actually did.

Some three months after these events, our boss Strugački sent the following email to everyone.

> Due to the lack of cohesion in our technical stack we decided to implement the following rules, for all new and existing repositories.

> 1. Unless there is a reasonable reason against doing so, all code should be written in Java.
> ...

The rest of the rules were just filler. And the condition "for all new and existing repositories" was also filler (everything except my project was already written in Java). They just wanted to make me ditch Haskell.

So, apparently, although he had always been with me on most technical matters, and was definitely much better off with me as his boss than with anyone else, Mirko had decided to get me fired, just out of spite.

He had carefully collected some of my actions, which, taken out of context, could appear irresponsible, and used them to tell Strugački a story about how I was doing whatever I pleased and how Anhela was covering for me and silencing all complaints about my work. He did all this in an awfully cunning way, but he didn't quite anticipate the boss's reaction: Strugački didn't particularly care that all of this was happening, as long as the product was delivered, which it was. The one thing that made him concerned was the fact that there weren't many people who knew Haskell besides me , which would make me hard to replace. And, with one email, he took care of that issue.

I met with Anhela right after receiving the email, and she immediately asked me about the rules—Strugački had mentioned to her that there were reports of her covering for me and this was enough for her to freak out.

"We have to split business and personal. I won't tolerate any more of your stuff."
{: .voice-anhela}

She knew very well that I was the real target, and that her position was not in danger. Still, she was eager to show Strugački that she was loyal to him, and so she began nagging me, without thinking about how this could affect our relationship. She kept asking me:

"So when are you going to rewrite that project of yours?"
{: .voice-anhela}

"It will be done this evening!"
{: .voice-x}

When you believe in what you say, you sound convincing, no matter how unlikely it is that you are right.
{: .note}

---

I knew that as soon as I started porting my beautiful Haskell code to Java I would feel miserable, at least for the rest of the day, and so I intentionally waited for the last hour of the day in which I actually could get it done. I even waited for Anhela to fall asleep, because I felt disgusted by her. And as I typed, my mind began reflecting on the whole situation:

On one hand was the mistake of believing that everyone else held the same values as me, that all the people around me shared my enthusiasm. So, even though they didn't work with me, I thought that Anhela and Strugački somehow participated in what I did. That they were at least cheering for me to succeed. But that wasn't the case: Anhela was just moving along the path of least resistance. And Strugački only cared about turning my work into money (his money).

But when you dig a little deeper, all that was secondary: the real problem was that *I* had become like them. I took a part of myself, a part of my REAL self, on my journey inside the machine's shell. My passion for programming, my friends, my love life were no longer completely mine.

By supposedly using the machine to achieve my goals, I had become a part of the machine, my goals had become its goals, and I had become a cog. And (although few people think like me) nobody cares—it makes no difference if a cog has opinions, ideals, or whether it is satisfied with being a cog, etc. The machine only cares if it is spinning.

Avoid being too melodramatic (but also don't hide your emotions).
{: .note}

I mean, you can try as you may to spin it any other way, but...

...there is no "true self" that you can assign to some of the things you did while writing off the rest as "just coping" and "just doing my job". Even if all people in the world are like you and even if all people in the world think you did good and you are cool and you have nothing to worry about, that is just their opinion. And even the strongest opinion doesn't make up for a weak character. I could COMPLAIN all day long about how miserable my life was, I could THEORIZE how what I do is not so bad, I could try to explain away what I did, but none of this would make me feel any better. 

There as only one thing that would make me better: to get drunk with Curious and delete the whole project (that has happened before, remember?)
